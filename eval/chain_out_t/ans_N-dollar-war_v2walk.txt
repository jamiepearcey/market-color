The US dollar's roughly 3% rise this year has been driven largely by shifting Federal Reserve expectations: a more hawkish-than-expected FOMC dot plot and Kevin Warsh's hawkish debut upended bets for rate cuts, fueling a surge in rate-hike expectations (now 32 bps of tightening priced by year-end, with a 29% chance of a July hike and 62% for September) as inflation runs above target and the economy keeps growing [1][3][19][20][13]. Optimism about the U.S. economy — including an AI-driven boom in U.S. equities that has drawn capital rapidly and prompted asset allocation toward U.S. assets — has added further support [3][5]. Safe-haven demand during the U.S.-Israeli conflict with Iran also contributed to the gains [8]. The dollar has additionally benefited from a widening U.S.-Japan interest rate differential, pushing the yen to its weakest since 1986 [12].

What the context does not provide is a single figure explicitly attributing the exact 3% year-to-date move to each driver; item [8] states the ~3% rise and cites safe-haven demand "partly," while the Fed-related and equity-inflows drivers are documented mainly around June/quarterly moves rather than a precise year-to-date breakdown [3][6][13].
