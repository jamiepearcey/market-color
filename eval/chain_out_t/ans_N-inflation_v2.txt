Inflation has risen well above central banks' 2% targets due to several reinforcing drivers. A key factor has been energy shocks from the Middle East conflict, which raised prices through gradual and complex cost transmission across the economy, with markets pricing structurally higher energy costs for years [3]; higher energy and food prices also forced advanced-economy central banks to reconsider rate cuts [10]. Demand-side pressure is also cited: "insatiable" demand for AI infrastructure from hyperscalers willing to pay almost any price is flagged as an inflation source [1][19], alongside a general lack of economic restraint among large companies amid a labor market near full employment and solid growth [1][9]. Core inflation has proven sticky and broad-based across the economy [8][11], and in Australia specifically inflation runs at 4.0% headline and 3.6% core—well above the 2–3% target—driven by excess demand and weak productivity growth [13]. Additional contributing factors include second-round effects [2] and fiscal risk shocks that lift inflation expectations alongside prices [15]. The context explains these drivers but does not give a single unified quantification of each factor's relative contribution to the overall overshoot.
